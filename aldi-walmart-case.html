ALDI VS. WALMART

Walmart takes about 21% of every US grocery dollar. Aldi takes 2.8%, but it is opening more than 180 stores this year and plans to reach 3,200 by 2028. The question is not who is bigger. It is who the next value shopper visits first.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY (BLUF)

The problem

Aldi is growing its US store count fast, from 2,361 locations in early 2024 to 2,614 at the end of 2025, with nearly 2,800 planned by the end of 2026. It reported 17 million new customers in 2025, and much of its new growth lands in the Southeast through converted Winn-Dixie and Harveys stores.

Why solve it

For Walmart, grocery is the weekly trip that brings shoppers into a Supercenter. A household that moves that trip to a small Aldi box also stops walking past everything else Walmart sells. Aldi's model is cheaper to run per store, so it can keep opening locations near Walmart's 3,560 Supercenters.

The answer

Walmart should not try to out-simplify Aldi. It should defend the trip where its model wins: online pickup and delivery, a sharper private label price ladder on key staples, and smaller Neighborhood Market formats in the markets where Aldi is converting stores.

I. TWO MODELS

Walmart wins grocery through scale. As of April 30, 2025 it ran 4,606 Walmart US stores, including 3,560 Supercenters averaging close to 178,000 square feet each.4 A Supercenter puts groceries next to general merchandise, so the weekly food trip pulls shoppers past everything else the store sells.

Aldi wins through simplicity. More than 90% of its assortment is its own private label, and its stores carry a short list of items rather than tens of thousands.2 Fewer items mean fewer suppliers to manage, faster restocking and a smaller box that is cheaper to build and staff. Margin through simplicity. A narrow assortment built on private label lets Aldi buy in very large volume per item and control its own product specifications. Products are displayed in their shipping cartons, and the store format is small and repeatable, so each new location is quick to open. The trade-off is choice: shoppers looking for a specific national brand often still need a second stop.

Scale through volume and convenience. Walmart spreads its supply chain over thousands of large stores and a broad assortment, and adds online pickup and delivery on top of the same stores. Groceries are the reason to visit often; general merchandise, pharmacy and services carry the margin. The trade-off is cost: a 178,000 square foot box is expensive to open, so Walmart adds few new Supercenters and relies on its existing base.

"Walmart sells the whole trip. Aldi sells the shortest trip."

II. THE EXPANSION

Aldi has turned a steady growth plan into one of the fastest store rollouts in US grocery.

Mar 2024

Aldi announces plans to add 800 US stores by the end of 2028, backed by more than $9 billion of investment over five years, starting from 2,361 locations.1

2024

Aldi acquires Southeastern Grocers, owner of Winn-Dixie and Harveys, and begins converting stores to the Aldi format across the Southeast.3

2025

Aldi reports 17 million new customers and says one in three US households shopped at Aldi during the year.3 It ends the year with 2,614 US stores.2

Jan 2026

Aldi plans more than 180 new stores across 31 states in 2026, including close to 80 Southeastern Grocers conversions, reaching nearly 2,800 stores by year end and 3,200 by the end of 2028.3

III. THE FOOTPRINT

Store count shows the gap Aldi is closing. Aldi's path rises from 2,361 stores to a 3,200 store target, while Walmart's Supercenter base holds near 3,560. By 2028 Aldi would run almost as many stores as Walmart has Supercenters, in a box a small fraction of the size.

Aldi 2024 and 2025 are reported counts; 2026 and 2028 are company targets. Walmart Supercenters as of April 30, 2025.

Share tells the other half. Walmart holds about 21% of US grocery spending and Aldi about 2.8%, based on Numerator data through early October 2025.2 Aldi is not taking Walmart's position; it is taking specific trips from specific households, one new store at a time.

V. INSIGHTS

01

Aldi competes for trips, not share.

At 2.8% of grocery spending, Aldi is far smaller than Walmart. Its threat is that each new store pulls the weekly staples trip from nearby households, and that trip is what brings shoppers into a Supercenter.

02

Store count is Aldi's real lever.

A small, repeatable box lets Aldi add more than 180 stores in a year. Walmart's large format is expensive to replicate, so its answer has to come from the stores and channels it already has.

03

Private label is the price engine.

With more than 90% of its assortment under its own brands, Aldi controls cost and specification on the items it sells. Walmart's best counter on price is its own private label on the same staples.

04

The Southeast is the front line.

Converting Winn-Dixie and Harveys stores gives Aldi ready-made sites in markets where Walmart is strong, faster than building new stores.

Recommendation

Walmart should defend the weekly trip rather than match Aldi's format. Price: hold a visible private label price gap on the 100 or so staples that drive a basket. Convenience: push pickup and delivery in neighborhoods within a short drive of new Aldi stores. Format: use Neighborhood Markets, already 671 stores, as the smaller-box answer in Southeast conversion markets. Measure: track trip frequency, not only share, in trade areas around each Aldi opening.

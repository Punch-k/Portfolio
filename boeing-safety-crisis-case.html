BOEING: SAFETY CRISIS

Boeing delivered 806 commercial jets in 2018 and 157 in 2020. Seven years later it still has not returned to that volume. A quality system that leaned on the regulator to find its problems turned a safety failure into an output failure.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY (BLUF)

The problem

Two 737 MAX crashes in October 2018 and March 2019 led to a worldwide grounding. Then on January 5, 2024 a door plug blew out of an Alaska Airlines 737 MAX 9; four bolts meant to hold it were missing when the jet left the factory. A follow-up FAA audit found failures in manufacturing process control, parts handling and storage, and product control.

Why solve it

Quality failures became capacity limits. Deliveries fell from 806 in 2018 to 157 in 2020, recovered to 528 in 2023, then dropped to 348 in 2024 after the FAA capped 737 MAX output at 38 a month. Every month spent below the cap is revenue and airline trust that competitors collect.

The answer

Make verified process control the gate for every rate increase. Put safety on the board agenda, trace every flight-critical step on the line, and raise output only once process capability is proven. That is the path the FAA took when it allowed 42 a month in October 2025.

I. THE GOVERNANCE GAP

Between the Lion Air crash in October 2018 and the Ethiopian Airlines crash in March 2019, Boeing's board reportedly never formally discussed grounding the 737 MAX.1 Safety information from the shop floor did not reliably reach the top, and the board was not the forcing function that stopped a known-risk aircraft from flying.

Regulators share the blame. The US Department of Transportation's review describes an oversight system in which FAA management at times sided with Boeing over its own technical experts during the original MAX certification.2

II. THE FACTORY FLOOR

The door plug was not a design flaw. It was a step that was not done and not checked. Select a finding below.

A pattern, not an incident. The FAA's 2024 audit of Boeing and its fuselage supplier found problems in manufacturing process control, parts handling and storage, and product control.3 Findings across several areas point to a system that tolerated skipped steps, not to one bad shift.

Every critical step needs a record. When a bolt installation on a pressurized fuselage is not verified and traced with the same rigor as flight software, the line has quietly decided some steps matter less. In response, the FAA increased its inspectors on site and tied any rate increase to its own review.4

V. INSIGHTS

01

Quality failures become capacity limits.

The FAA cap turned a factory problem into a hard ceiling on output. The cost of weak process control showed up as hundreds of undelivered aircraft, not just as fines.

02

Governance has to pull safety signals up.

A board that does not see frontline safety issues cannot act on them. Safety needs its own committee, metrics and escalation path, separate from delivery targets.

03

Traceability is the cheapest control.

Recording that each critical step was done and checked costs far less than finding a missing bolt in flight.

04

Rate follows capability, not the other way round.

The rise to 42 a month came only after the FAA reviewed the lines. Linking each rate step to proven process capability rebuilds trust and output together.

Recommendation

Board: a standing safety committee with direct reporting from quality and whistleblower channels. Line: mandatory traceability on every flight-critical installation, checked by a second person. Rate: each production increase gated on measured process capability, agreed with the regulator in advance. Suppliers: the same standards and audits at the fuselage supplier as on Boeing's own lines.

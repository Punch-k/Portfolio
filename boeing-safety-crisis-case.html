BOEING: SAFETY CRISIS

A manufacturing crisis, in hindsight, is almost always a governance crisis first — Boeing's is a case study in what happens when internal audit stops being the safety net.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY (BLUF)

Boeing's 737 MAX crisis — from the 2018-2019 MCAS crashes through the January 2024 Alaska Airlines door-plug blowout — is not a single defect but a chain of governance failures: a board that reportedly never discussed grounding the MAX during the crash investigations, a reliance on FAA inspection instead of internal audit to catch problems, and a 2024 FAA audit that found ongoing quality-control failures in manufacturing process control, parts handling, and product control. This case traces how the breakdown moved from the boardroom down to a missing set of door-plug bolts on the factory floor.

I. THE GOVERNANCE GAP

Between November 2018 and March 2019 — the window between the Lion Air and Ethiopian Airlines 737 MAX crashes and the FAA's eventual grounding order — Boeing's board reportedly never formally considered or discussed grounding the aircraft on safety grounds. That is the detail that turns a "manufacturing story" into a governance one: the highest level of oversight in the company was not the forcing function that stopped a known-risk aircraft from flying.

Compounding this, whistleblowers reported operating-process problems that were reportedly ignored by management and not consistently escalated to the board — meaning the information gap wasn't just about what leadership didn't know, but about what frontline signal never made it up the chain at all.

"Boeing did not use internal audits to identify and correct operational problems — it relied on the FAA to find its safety issues for it."

— U.S. Senate Investigation Findings

II. QUALITY CONTROL BREAKDOWN

The clearest post-MAX evidence of the gap between governance intent and shop-floor reality came on January 5, 2024, when a door plug blew out mid-flight on an Alaska Airlines 737 MAX 9. The preliminary investigation found four key bolts that were supposed to secure the door plug were missing when the aircraft left Boeing's factory — not a design flaw, a process-control failure.

Multiple Instances of Failure: The FAA's subsequent audit found problems specifically in Boeing's manufacturing process control, parts handling and storage, and product control — a systemic pattern, not an isolated incident on one production line.

Traceability Gaps: When a door-plug bolt installation isn't verified and traced with the same rigor as flight-critical software, the quality system has effectively decided some steps matter less than others — the exact inversion of how a safety-critical manufacturing line should be run.

III. FAA OVERSIGHT FAILURE

This wasn't a one-sided failure. Multiple career FAA officials have documented cases where FAA management overruled its own technical experts at Boeing's request during the original MAX certification. The regulator's own retrospective conclusion was direct: Boeing failed in the design and development of the MAX, and the FAA failed in its oversight and certification of the aircraft. In response, the FAA increased onsite inspector presence at Boeing's Renton facility and at Spirit AeroSystems in Wichita, and halted MAX production-rate expansion.

IV. THE FIX

Post-2024, the FAA capped Boeing's 737 production rate and mandated an independent quality-control overhaul before any rate increase would be approved — deliberately forcing the fix to happen at the pace of verified process control, not at the pace of delivery targets. The broader governance lesson generalizes well beyond aerospace: an internal audit function that only reacts to what an external regulator finds first is not really a governance function — it's a compliance mirror.

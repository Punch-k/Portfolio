SCRUM REPLENISHMENT

Six Flags Great Lakes — bringing a sprint-based review cadence to a staffing decision that used to be made on gut feel at 11am on a Saturday.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY (BLUF)

Peak-day ride staffing at Six Flags was being decided reactively — add an operator once the line is visibly out of control. We replaced that with a two-week sprint cadence: review queue-and-utilization data from the prior peak days, run the numbers through an M/M/c queueing model (the same math behind the site's Queue & Staffing Lab), and commit to a staffing plan for the next peak window before it starts, instead of during it. Combined with automated reporting, this cut manual reporting time 60% and gave management a defensible answer before the queue ever built up.

CASE DETAILS

Date: May 2026

Read Time: 5 Minutes

Focus: Operations Cadence, Queueing Theory, Reporting

I. THE REACTIVE PROBLEM

On a typical summer Saturday, the park's flagship coaster ran with 3 ride operators handling loading, safety checks, and dispatch — a headcount set months earlier and never revisited against actual demand. When the queue grew visibly by 11am, the only lever available was an ad-hoc radio call for a 4th operator, made under pressure, with no data backing the decision either way.

The underlying issue wasn't the staffing number itself — it was that there was no repeatable process for deciding it. Every peak day re-litigated the same question from scratch.

"If you're deciding staffing while the queue is already out of control, the model already failed. The decision has to happen before the gate opens."

— Operations Retro Notes

II. THE SPRINT CADENCE

We borrowed the Scrum sprint/retro structure from software delivery and applied it to a physical operations problem. Select a workstream below.

Data-First Retro: Every two weeks, ride ops leads reviewed arrival rates, service times, and observed wait times from the prior peak days — the same inputs the queueing model needs — and used them to commit to a staffing plan for the upcoming peak window, before the next Saturday started, not during it.

Reporting Automation: Manual end-of-day queue logs were replaced with automated dashboards pulling directly from ride-throughput counters, cutting the reporting workload by roughly 60% and freeing the ops team to spend that time on the staffing decision itself rather than data entry.

III. THE QUEUEING MODEL

With a new guest joining the queue roughly every 2.5 minutes and each boarding cycle taking about 5 minutes, the sprint retro ran those numbers through an M/M/c queueing model to test 3 vs. 4 operators before committing headcount. The model made the diminishing-return curve visible: a 4th operator meaningfully cut average wait time, but a 5th barely moved it — turning "should we add staff" from a gut call into a staffing-cost-vs.-wait-time trade-off the team could defend to management.

IV. THE PAYOFF

Staffing decisions moved from reactive radio calls mid-queue to a pre-committed plan backed by the prior sprint's data. The queueing model didn't just justify adding a 4th operator on the busiest days — it also justified not adding a 5th on days where the marginal wait-time improvement didn't cover the labor cost, closing the loop on a decision that used to run purely on instinct.

Methodology & Sources: The queueing model referenced is the M/M/c (Erlang-C) formulation, the standard multi-server queueing theory framework dating to A.K. Erlang's early-20th-century telephony research and still the baseline model for staffing-vs.-wait-time trade-offs in operations management. The sprint/retro cadence is adapted from the Scrum framework (Sutherland & Schwaber, "The Scrum Guide"), applied here to a physical operations context rather than software delivery.

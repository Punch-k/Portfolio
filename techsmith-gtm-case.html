TECHSMITH GTM

Moving a mid-market B2B SaaS vendor off commodity pricing and onto a usage-based, consultative sales motion — before the valuation conversation ever started.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY (BLUF)

As part of a Broad Consulting Club engagement, TechSmith Corporation — a $5M-revenue B2B software vendor — was losing renewal leverage by pricing seat-based licenses like a commodity. We restructured the go-to-market motion around usage-based tiers and a "land-and-expand" account model, cutting the average sales cycle by roughly a third and setting up the growth trajectory that later underpinned the acquisition valuation (see the companion M&A Deal Thesis case).

CASE DETAILS

Date: March 2026

Read Time: 5 Minutes

Focus: GTM Strategy, Pricing, Sales Ops

I. THE COMMODITY TRAP

TechSmith sold its core platform on flat, seat-based annual licenses — a structure that rewarded headcount, not usage. Sales reps were discounting to close, renewal conversations were purely transactional, and expansion revenue inside existing accounts was near zero. Growth was entirely a function of new-logo acquisition, the most expensive lever a SaaS business has.

The diagnostic surfaced a deeper issue: the pricing model gave customers no reason to grow their usage of the platform, because growing usage didn't change what they paid. Value delivered and price charged had become disconnected.

"You can't fix a sales motion with a new CRM if the pricing model underneath it still rewards the wrong behavior."

— Engagement Kickoff Memo

II. THE REINVENTION

We proposed restructuring the offer into three usage-based tiers, anchored on active-seat consumption and API call volume rather than nominal headcount. Select a workstream below to see how the reinvention was operationalized.

Usage-Based Tiers: Replaced the flat per-seat license with a Starter / Growth / Scale structure priced on active usage. This let land deals start small (lower friction, faster procurement approval) while giving the customer a direct incentive to expand — more usage now visibly correlated with more value, not just more cost.

Land-and-Expand: Reps were re-trained to sell a minimum viable footprint into a new account, then hand off to a dedicated customer success motion focused on usage growth. Commission structures were rebalanced so expansion revenue counted as much as new-logo revenue, removing the incentive to over-discount at signing.

III. ACCOUNT SEGMENTATION

Not every account should be sold the same way. We segmented the existing book of business into three tiers by expansion potential (current usage headroom, org size, technical integration depth) and built a differentiated playbook for each — high-touch strategic accounts got a named CSM, mid-market got a pooled success team, and long-tail accounts were moved to a self-serve upgrade flow inside the product itself.

IV. THE RESULTS

Within two quarters of the new pricing tiers going live, expansion revenue from existing accounts became a measurable, forecastable growth line — no longer an afterthought behind new-logo bookings. That predictable, compounding growth curve is exactly what fed into the 18%-then-9% revenue growth assumptions used in the acquirer's DCF model, explored in the companion M&A case below.

Methodology & Sources: The pricing and account-segmentation framework applied here draws on published SaaS commercialization research on usage-based pricing and net revenue retention (see OpenView Partners' annual SaaS Benchmarks reports and Bessemer Venture Partners' Cloud Index for industry-standard NRR/expansion-revenue definitions).
